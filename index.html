ABOUT ME

12.26.2002

I study what happens when human beings — none of whom have the god's-eye view, none of whom can see the future — come together and try to build something that lasts. My work is grounded in psychology, sociology, and decision-making. My undergraduate thesis, Surviving the Cut: Understanding Emotional Responses and Social Behavior Among Layoff Survivors, came from a place of real urgency — my parents were both laid off after more than 25 years at the same company, a place where they had met each other, during the disruptions of Covid-19. I designed and ran the study myself, navigated IRB approval independently, pursued company partnerships, and carried the project to completion through real grief, including the loss of a close friend during the final stretch. I received a competitive summer fellowship to support the research. The thesis is imperfect, I'll be the first to say so. It is also entirely mine.

My intellectual lens is shaped by having grown up across Shanghai, Taipei, Tokyo, and the American Midwest — a path that made me permanently attentive to how culture shapes the way institutions think and fail. I'm drawn to the questions where structure meets behavior: principal-agent problems, institutional theory, population ecology, and the mechanisms by which organizations catalyze radical change or quietly generate the friction that destroys them. I approach these questions with curiosity first, and rigor second — in that order intentionally.

For four years, I worked as a barista while building my academic record, funding much of my education through scholarships I applied for myself, far from family. I'm now preparing to pursue doctoral study in management, organizational behavior, or strategy — seeking programs where I can contribute to understanding how bounded, imperfect, culturally embedded human beings build organizations worth belonging to.
